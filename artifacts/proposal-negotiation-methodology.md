# Proposal and Negotiation Methodology

## A decision document built from evidence
A proposal is neither a price sheet, feature list, architecture, brochure, company presentation, nor contract. It makes a good decision understandable: problem, importance, recommendation and rationale, boundary, investment, uncertainty, responsibilities, and next action. Use plain business language and concise sections rather than technical jargon.

Every assertion must retain lineage to Chapters 8–15: discovery, current workflow, problem economics, qualification, solution choice, solution economics, scope, and pricing. Copy the bounded problem and scope rather than rewriting them into sales language. An exclusion cannot become a promise. MEASURED, ESTIMATED, HYPOTHETICAL, and UNKNOWN evidence cannot be silently upgraded. Confirm customer price against the selected pricing analysis.

## Practical assembly
Use an executive summary; current situation and validated problem; why it matters; recommended approach and alternatives; business outcome and included/excluded scope; three-party responsibilities; assumptions/dependencies; acceptance and business metrics; investment/payment; risks; options; next steps; and a validity window. A validity period is contextual, not universal: cost, availability, assumptions, and vendor pricing can change.

Recommend CONFIGURE, INTEGRATE, AUTOMATE, CUSTOM_BUILD, or LEAVE_ALONE only as evidence permits. If capability remains unknown, sell a paid validation phase rather than pretending implementation is settled. Identify an unselected delivery path honestly. Separate delivery acceptance from business success. Separate base price from recurring third-party costs, options, and support.

## Revisions preserve history
Never overwrite an earlier offer. Version each changed scope, price, payment term, assumption, and reason. Log each customer request, its effects, response alternatives, decision, and whether a revision or walk-away follows.

## Negotiation is not automatic discounting
Evaluate customer need, business value, scope, cost, owner time, customer economics, Local Works economics, delivery risk, cash exposure, and relationship impact. Available levers include price, scope, timing, phases, payment, options, delivery approach, risk allocation, start date, and support.

A discount leaves fixed delivery cost in place. For $6,000 revenue and $3,000 direct cost, a 20% cut creates $4,800 revenue and $1,800 contribution: price falls 20% while contribution falls 40%. Compare a genuinely re-costed smaller scope or phased engagement rather than assuming discount and scope reduction are equivalent.

When someone quoted less, professionally compare what each quote covers: discovery, workflow analysis, validation, coordination, QA, acceptance, documentation, support, third-party costs, and ownership. The other quote may be better. Explain differences, narrow Local Works' role, let the customer choose, or walk away; never disparage the competitor.

For insufficient budget, consider smaller scope, later start, customer-owned internal work, phases, leave-alone, or decline—not invented financing or pressure. Added work is repriced, made optional, deferred, or excluded. A no-deposit demand is evaluated against supplier timing and maximum cash exposure. Unsupported ROI guarantees are declined when adoption, volume, policy, behavior, employee use, or external platforms determine results; demonstrable delivery criteria may still be commitments. An impossible deadline calls for reduced scope, validation, phases, an alternative, or refusal—not wishful estimating.

## Healthy exits and lifecycle boundaries
Walk away when price is unprofitable, payment protection is refused, claims must be dishonest, scope stays intentionally vague, deadlines are impossible, authority is unclear, a critical capability is absent, security/delivery risk is unacceptable, material constraints must be hidden, the relationship is abusive, or economics collapse. A lost sale can be a successful decision.

ACCEPTED_IN_PRINCIPLE is only a commercial response. It does not mean contract executed, deposit received, project started, technical delivery authorized, or business outcomes guaranteed. Those commitment controls begin in Chapter 17.

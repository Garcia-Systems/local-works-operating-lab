# Chapter 16 — Proposal and Negotiation

> **Core question:** How does Local Works turn qualified, scoped, economically healthy work into a clear customer decision without destroying the deal?

## 1. Proposal is a decision document
The purpose is not to impress. A proposal makes a good decision easy to understand. It is not merely a price sheet, feature list, architecture, sales brochure, generic presentation, or contract. A reader should understand the problem, importance, recommendation, rationale, exact boundary, price, uncertainty, responsibilities, and next step.

## 2. Build from evidence
The proposal is downstream of Chapters 8–15. Discovery supports the workflow; workflow supports burden; qualification supports continued effort; alternatives support the approach; solution economics support careful benefits; scope supplies boundaries; pricing supplies investment. Cite that lineage. Never promote UNKNOWN retention to “20% improvement,” or an exclusion into a feature.

## 3. Executive summary
Use plain business language: current repeated handling, the simplest supported intervention, and the bounded freeze workflow. Avoid architecture vocabulary unless it explains value.

## 4. Explain the current situation
Describe workflow, friction, affected people, frequency, burden, evidence quality, and constraints without saying a process is “broken.” “Appears to require repeated handling” is accurate when evidence is incomplete.

## 5. State the problem
A useful statement is specific, evidenced, bounded, and business-oriented: routine eligible freeze requests require staff intervention and customer follow-up—not “modernize the website.”

## 6. Explain why it matters
Use supported labor, rework, delay, error, inconvenience, capacity, and economics. Keep MEASURED, ESTIMATED, HYPOTHETICAL, and UNKNOWN visible. Soft value does not become fake dollars.

## 7. Recommend an approach
Name CONFIGURE, INTEGRATE, AUTOMATE, CUSTOM_BUILD, or LEAVE_ALONE. If capability is unknown, recommend a **paid validation phase**. The offer must not outrun evidence.

## 8. Explain alternatives
Say why the path is preferable. Configuration may reduce complexity; a custom portal may be premature. Considering alternatives demonstrates judgment, not indecision.

## 9. Carry scope forward
Retain business outcome, trigger, end condition, inclusions, exclusions, MUSTs, and relevant SHOULDs. Options are explicitly OPTIONAL, DEFERRED, or FUTURE_DISCOVERY and excluded from base price unless stated.

## 10. Price and payment
Show customer price—not irrelevant internal cost detail—separately from recurring vendors, options, and future support. State deposit, milestones/final amount, and due events. No billing occurs here.

## 11. Assumptions and responsibilities
State meaningful assumptions, dependencies, and consequences. Divide customer responsibilities, Local Works coordination/oversight, and an approved but potentially unselected delivery path. Do not imply Local Works personally implements delegated technical work.

## 12. Proposal versioning
Give every revision a version. Preserve changed scope, price, payment, assumptions, and reason. A validity window protects against changed availability, cost, vendor price, and assumptions; 30 days is an example, not a standard.

## 13. Negotiation is not discounting
A concern is not an instruction to lower price. Evaluate customer need, value, scope, cost, owner time, both sides' economics, delivery risk, cash exposure, and relationship. Then accept, counter, reduce scope, phase, change payment, defer, validate, decline, or walk away.

## 14. “Your price is too high”
Check whether value evidence is weaker, budget lower, scope broad, a simpler path exists, phases help, or the outcome is unclear. A modest discount is only one option and must remain healthy.

## 15. “Someone quoted less”
Compare boundaries: discovery, workflow, validation, coordination, QA, acceptance, documentation, support, third-party costs, and ownership. Do not attack the developer. Their offer may genuinely be better; explain differences, narrow the role, let the customer choose, or leave.

## 16. “Can you add this?”
Cancellation is not silently folded into freezes. Add and reprice it, make it optional, defer it to Phase 2, or keep it excluded. Sales is where scope creep can begin.

## 17. “We don't have the budget”
Offer smaller scope, phases, later start, or customer-owned internal portions where sensible. Leave alone or decline when those do not work. Do not manufacture financing or pressure a bad deal.

## 18. “Can we pay after launch?”
Map supplier startup payments and all pre-receipt costs. Counter when Local Works cannot finance the maximum exposure. Revenue and contribution do not eliminate cash timing.

## 19. “Guarantee the ROI”
Do not guarantee results controlled by adoption, customer behavior, volume, policy, employees, or vendors. Commit where appropriate to observable delivery acceptance; preserve uncertain economics.

## 20. “We need it faster”
Urgency cannot rewrite reality. Reduce scope, validate first, phase launch, use another path, or decline the deadline. A technical estimate comes later.

## 21. Discount mathematics
A fictional $10,000 project with $6,000 direct cost has $4,000 contribution. A 25% cut produces $7,500 revenue and only $1,500 contribution. With $2,000 owner-time value, owner-adjusted contribution is **-$500**. **Do not accept unchanged scope at this price.** Reduce/re-cost scope, phase, simplify, restore price, or decline.

## 22. Phased deals
Phase 1 can validate capability; Phase 2 proceeds only if evidence supports implementation. This reduces commitment and Local Works risk, improves estimates and evidence, and enables an easier stop decision. It is a successful structure, not failure to sell a larger job.

## 23. Walk-away conditions
Walk away from unprofitable price, refusal of necessary payment protection, dishonest claims, deliberately vague scope, impossible deadlines, unclear authority, unavailable critical capability, unacceptable security/delivery risk, hidden constraints, abusive relationships, or collapsed economics. A lost sale can be a successful decision.

## 24. Harbor Fitness negotiation
Version 1 offers the bounded freeze workflow for a fictional $6,000 and 50/50 payment. Harbor compares a lower quote, requests a discount, cancellation, no deposit, and guaranteed ROI. Local Works respectfully compares boundaries, declines unchanged-scope discount, phases validation, defers cancellation, retains payment protection, and rejects an economic guarantee. Version 2 is accepted in principle for Phase 1. The deal remains conditionally healthy.

A failure contrast starts with one workflow and adds reporting, mobile access, migration, and an integration at the same price. Each addition raises delivery and owner exposure while revenue stays fixed. Economics collapse before contracting: **scope creep can begin during sales**.

## 25. Acceptance is not yet a project
Proposal sent is not a sale. ACCEPTED_IN_PRINCIPLE is not a legal contract, deposit, project start, delivery authorization, or guaranteed outcome. Outcomes may also be revision, postponement, decline, expiration, Local Works withdrawal, or no healthy deal.

The reader can assemble evidence, state a bounded problem, explain an approach, preserve boundaries and uncertainty, present price/payment, distinguish acceptance from commitment, calculate discount effects, answer competitor/addition/budget/payment/ROI/deadline requests professionally, phase work, identify walk-away conditions, and preserve a healthy deal.

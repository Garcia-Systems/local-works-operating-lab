# Chapter 18 — Find the Delivery Path

> **Part V — Assemble the Delivery System**
>
> **Core question:** Who should perform the technical work, and how can Local Works choose a path without depending on the wrong partner?

The progression is **authorized opportunity → delivery needs → delivery options → candidate paths → fit → risk → delivery-path decision → ready for technical estimate**. Chapter 17's Harbor Fitness record is still blocked, so assessment may continue but implementation does not begin.

## 1. The delivery partner is not the product

Local Works sells problem understanding, bounded scope, solution and commercial judgment, customer leadership, coordination, documentation continuity, QA coordination, and support coordination. Technical capability can come from several paths. Losing one provider must not erase customer history, requirements, source, credentials, decisions, deployment knowledge, or supportability.

## 2. The three-party model

The **customer** owns the business problem and decisions. **Local Works** owns the customer relationship, translation, solution leadership, coordination, and commercial accountability. The **delivery team** performs some or all implementation. Local Works is neither a reseller made irrelevant after an introduction nor a rule that one developer must do everything. Specialists may speak directly with stakeholders when useful while Local Works continues doing substantive leadership work.

## 3. Define delivery needs first

Start with solution and scope, not a favorite provider. Classify required and optional configuration, API, workflow automation, front-end, back-end, database, migration, cloud, security, QA, UX, accessibility, platform, documentation, handoff, and scale capabilities. Record importance, provenance, notes, and `UNKNOWN`. Needs describe the engagement; candidate capabilities describe evidence about a candidate. They are different records.

## 4. Delivery paths

Possible paths are Local Works self-delivery, independent contractor, specialist freelancer, small or larger agency, platform implementation partner, vendor professional services, customer internal team, mixed team, and no external work. Configuration may need no developer; integration may need a specialist; a substantial custom build may warrant a team. Geography is not a proxy for cost or quality, and no path is inherently best.

## 5. Self delivery

Self-delivery retains context and control and avoids partner margin. It can also consume owner capacity, expose skill gaps, distract from leadership, create support burden, and concentrate knowledge in one person. “No supplier invoice” does not mean zero cost.

## 6. Contractors

Independent contractors can be flexible, specialized, and cost-effective. Availability, capacity, disappearance, documentation, limited backup, and handoff require explicit inspection. Solo status is a risk factor to manage, not an automatic rejection.

## 7. Agencies

Agencies can offer process, multiple disciplines, QA, and backup people. They may have higher cost, communication layers, less flexibility, or a minimum engagement size. Team size does not prove fit or reliability.

## 8. Vendor services

Vendor professional services or implementation partners may possess direct platform knowledge. Pricing, limited customization, platform incentives, and vendor dependency still matter.

## 9. Customer internal teams

Internal teams know customer systems and can preserve customer ownership. They may face competing priorities, capacity limits, or skill gaps. “Available” is not “able to allocate enough time.”

## 10. Mixed teams

A mixed team can combine Local Works context, internal access, and specialist capability. It also raises coordination and ownership ambiguity. Name decision, communication, access, and accountability boundaries.

## 11. Capability fit

Assess technical fit, platform and integration experience, security awareness, testing, documentation, communication, availability, capacity, reliability, handoff, and cost fit as `STRONG`, `ADEQUATE`, `UNCERTAIN`, `WEAK`, or `NO_FIT`. Avoid a magic score. A strong React developer can be wrong for an accounting integration; an automation expert can be wrong for a large application. **Good technician does not equal right delivery fit.**

## 12. Evidence

Preserve `SELF_REPORTED`, `PUBLIC_PORTFOLIO`, `REFERENCE`, `DIRECT_EVALUATION`, `PAST_PERFORMANCE`, `CERTIFICATION`, and `UNKNOWN`. A claim is not verification, a portfolio is not observed Local Works performance, and references retain their source. Do not invent background checks or delivery history.

## 13. Cost is only one dimension

A fictional $1,500 contractor with no documentation, personal-only repository and accounts, inconsistent communication, and no handoff may create costly rework and recovery. A $3,000 provider offering shared source, documentation, predictable communication, and handoff reduces some risks. Neither automatically wins. Compare requirements, assumptions, evidence, rework exposure, continuity, and total risk—not price alone.

## 14. Communication and reliability

Look for predictable response, useful questions, explained tradeoffs, surfaced uncertainty, documentation willingness, and healthy escalation. Reliability evidence includes commitments met, delays raised early, realistic estimates, consistent meetings, and recorded decisions. Do not equate accent, nationality, geography, or superficial fluency with technical quality.

## 15. Availability vs capacity

A person may be available for five hours per week while the project needs twenty. Record start/window availability separately from sustained capacity. Both may remain unknown.

## 16. Source and account control

For custom work, prefer a repository controlled appropriately by the customer and/or Local Works under the agreement, rather than a contractor's personal account holding the only source. This is operational continuity, not a legal ownership declaration.

Hosting, domain, automation, monitoring, email, cloud, and API services should not exist only under a contractor's private identity when continuity requires customer or Local Works control. Record **account owner, administrative access, and recovery path**. Never collect real passwords in assessment records. Prefer customer-controlled accounts, separate least-privilege users, temporary/revocable access, and project-appropriate secret management.

## 17. Documentation and QA

Scale setup, environment/deployment, integration, configuration, known limitation, troubleshooting, and support-handoff notes to complexity. Trivial work does not need a manual, but important knowledge must transfer. “Developer says it works” is not acceptance. Assess test practices, reproducibility, environments, defect correction, and acceptance support; QA execution remains later.

## 18. Security/access

For sensitive work consider minimized access, customer data handling, credentials, logging, dependency risk, vulnerability awareness, and incident escalation. This prequalification is neither a security certification nor permission to expose credentials.

## 19. Subcontracting

Record `NONE`, `POSSIBLE`, `EXPECTED`, or `UNKNOWN`. Ask who performs work, gets access, communicates, remains accountable, and whether customer notice or approval is required. Subcontracting is not inherently bad; invisible material subcontracting is unmanaged risk.

## 20. Partner incentives

A custom-development agency may earn more from recommending custom work. Local Works must preserve the Chapter 12 order—configure, integrate, automate, custom build, leave alone—independently. A partner's business model cannot become the customer's solution strategy.

## 21. Key-person risk

If one person alone knows code, deployment, credentials, architecture, and vendor setup, disappearance can stop delivery. Mitigate through shared repositories and account control, milestone documentation, decision logs, incremental handoff, Local Works knowledge retention, and a transition plan. Do not reject capable solo providers solely for being solo.

## 22. Backup paths

Important engagements deserve a primary and contingency path, but not necessarily two paid teams. Transition-ready documentation and controls can allow another qualified provider to continue. With provider-only source and credentials, disappearance is catastrophic; with shared source, accounts, decisions, tests, and deployment notes, it remains painful but manageable.

## 23. Local Works as the relationship owner

The customer should not accidentally have to manage Local Works's contractor. Local Works consolidates business communication, translates decisions, manages expectations, and owns the commercial relationship. This does not hide technical people or prohibit direct technical discussion; it preserves Local Works's actual contribution.

## 24. Local Works as a good client

Local Works owes partners clear scope, prompt answers and decisions, consolidated feedback, controlled changes, protection from contradictory instructions, and payment on agreed terms. It should not demand indefinite unpaid exploration. Unknown legacy systems, APIs, migrations, or vendor access may justify paid technical discovery before a responsible estimate.

## 25. Harbor Fitness delivery options

Chapter 17 left Harbor Fitness accepted in principle but blocked on authority, agreement, payment, capability/API validation, and access. The current direction is configuration-first validation for the check-in workflow—not a full custom team. Required needs are platform configuration, workflow analysis, testing, documentation/handoff, and Local Works coordination; API investigation is contingent.

The fictional field contains a platform specialist, integration freelancer, small agency, Local Works self-delivery, and customer internal IT. The specialist best matches configuration but has narrow integration depth and solo risk. The integration freelancer has strong API evidence but uncertain platform fit. The agency has broad custom skill and QA but is oversized and commercially inclined toward custom work. Local Works retains context but lacks confirmed platform capacity. Internal IT offers access but insufficient capacity.

## 26. Failure: cheapest provider

Selecting the $1,500 option solely on price ignores personal-only source and accounts, poor communication, and absent handoff. Automatically choosing the $3,000 option is also unjustified. Prequalification exposes risk; Chapter 19 compares like-for-like estimates.

## 27. Failure: disappearing contractor

In the bad setup, source sits on a laptop, deployment is unknown, customer credentials are held privately, and decisions are undocumented. Recovery is extremely difficult. In the better setup, shared source, customer/Local Works administrative control, milestone documentation, a decision log, and test evidence survive. Recovery is manageable, though never free.

## 28. Success: right-sized delivery

A validated simple configuration problem may need six hours from a platform specialist, not custom software. The customer gets a timely outcome while Local Works preserves documentation and the relationship. Sophistication means appropriate capability, not maximum complexity.

## 29. Qualified-for-estimate gate

Prequalify relevant experience and evidence, stack/platform, typical project size, availability, capacity, relationship model, communication, documentation, handoff, security/access, repository/accounts, subcontracting, and commercial expectations. Outcomes are `QUALIFIED_FOR_ESTIMATE`, `NEEDS_MORE_INFORMATION`, `NOT_QUALIFIED`, `NOT_AVAILABLE`, `WRONG_DELIVERY_MODEL`, or `TOO_RISKY`. Only the first proceeds to technical estimate comparison.

Harbor Fitness's fictional Northstar specialist and Bridge integration freelancer qualify for appropriately bounded estimate requests. Cedar is the wrong model now; Local Works needs more information; internal IT is unavailable as lead. This set is not a winner, appointment, outreach, estimate, kickoff, or authorization.

The reader can explain the three parties; distinguish path from contractor; derive needs from scope; compare self, contractor, specialist, agency, vendor, customer, mixed, and no-external-work paths; assess fit and evidence; compare cost with risk; assess communication, reliability, availability, and capacity; expose key-person, source, account, credential, security, subcontracting, incentive, documentation, QA, and handoff risks; protect relationship and continuity; behave as a good partner client; and identify only the candidates ready to receive technical estimate requests.

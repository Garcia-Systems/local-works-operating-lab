# Chapter 11 — Qualify the Opportunity

> All examples, including Harbor Fitness, are fictional training material. They are not Local Works results.

## 1. A real problem can still be a bad deal

A real workflow problem and real economic burden are necessary evidence, not a sales conclusion. The customer may not care now. A local contact may lack authority. Change may be contractually impossible. The need may belong with a specialist. The engagement may demand unsafe conduct. Local Works must be willing to say **do not pursue**.

## 2. The qualification gate

Part III now moves through **Digital Friction Audit → Opportunity Gate → Discovery → Current-State Workflow → Problem Economics → Qualification Decision**. Chapter 8 asked what is happening, Chapter 9 how work happens, and Chapter 10 what burden it creates. This chapter consumes those records and asks: **should Local Works invest another increment of sales and solution-design effort?** It does not rediscover the whole workflow and uses no opaque lead score.

A matrix keeps problem understanding, economics, priority, urgency, authority, capacity, technical plausibility, organizational feasibility, measurability, fit, and commercial risk independent. Its output names positive signals, concerns, unknowns, hard disqualifiers, a decision, rationale, and next action.

## 3. Problem understanding

A strong opportunity is framed around business work rather than a requested feature. It identifies a workflow, people affected, major exceptions, policies, constraints, and contradictions. “We need AI” is weak until evidence establishes the work underneath. Unresolved critical contradictions call for more evidence, not optimism.

## 4. Economic significance

Import Chapter 10's result without upgrading its certainty. Economically trivial burden favors decline. Potentially meaningful burden differs from meaningful burden established by stronger evidence. `MORE_EVIDENCE_REQUIRED` remains exactly that. Do not invent retention, goodwill, or revenue to save a marginal opportunity, and never confuse current burden with recoverable solution value.

## 5. Does the customer care?

Analytical importance is not customer priority. If management acknowledges substantial time but excludes change from this year's priorities, nurture or stop for now. This respects the customer's choices and prevents premature proposal labor.

## 6. Why now?

Growth, workload, complaints, replacement or renewal, expansion, a new requirement, a deadline, or a management initiative can establish urgency. “No particular reason” is honest evidence: not automatically disqualifying, but relevant to sales priority. Local Works must not manufacture a deadline.

## 7. Authority

Map only the roles the situation requires: user, influencer, champion, decision maker, budget owner, technical approver, and procurement/other approval. In an owner-operated firm one person may fill several. At a corporate location, a local manager may be helpful yet unable to change technology. A plausible authorized path matters more than an impressive title.

## 8. Budget without guessing

`UNKNOWN` is not `INSUFFICIENT`. Capacity may be known, plausible, unknown, insufficient, or paired with unwillingness to invest. Instead of declaring what a customer can afford, learn whether it already pays for business systems, whether management would invest if value is established, when budgets are set, who controls spending, what approval level applies, and whether this need can compete with other investments. Bound pre-sales effort while capacity remains unresolved.

## 9. Technical plausibility

Qualification asks whether *some* realistic improvement path appears possible—perhaps process change, configuration, integration, automation, or software—and what platform/API/access constraint needs investigation. It does not choose among paths. Technically possible is not economically sensible.

## 10. Organizational feasibility

A feasible technology can meet an immovable organization. Corporate or franchise control, vendor contracts, policy refusal, regulation, access, and adoption can prevent change. Ask whether the organization can actually act, not merely whether code can be written.

## 11. Measurability

Could the team eventually observe change—for example minutes per request, manual transfers, or follow-up rate—with real before/after evidence? Do not invent baselines or targets. Valuable outcomes can be difficult to measure, so uncertainty here informs judgment rather than automatically rejecting the work.

## 12. Local Works fit

Good fit combines business workflow friction, a technology contribution, business/technical translation, a responsible delivery path, and a manageable relationship. Primarily branding, accounting, legal, emergency cybersecurity, commodity break/fix, massive enterprise transformation, or unsupported regulated specialization is weak fit. `REFER_ELSEWHERE` is a useful professional outcome.

## 13. Commercial risk

Statements such as “price it without questions,” “tomorrow,” contingent payment, unlimited changes, unknown approval, or “don't tell corporate” deserve context. Classify them as concern, major concern, or disqualifier rather than treating every awkward statement as bad faith. Disciplined recognition protects both parties; cynicism does not.

## 14. Hard disqualifiers

Illegal or unethical aims, deliberate deception, unsafe expectations, unacceptable access, abusive conditions, no authorized path, demanded guarantees, or capability gaps without responsible delivery paths are boundaries. Strong economics, authority, or priority cannot average them away. A hard disqualifier produces `DISQUALIFY`.

## 15. Protecting owner time

Additional discovery, vendor research, technical investigation, proposal work, and partner sourcing consume finite owner capacity. A marginal opportunity requiring 20 unpaid hours competes with a clearer one requiring three. Estimate remaining effort before pricing and approve only a bounded next increment. Qualification is opportunity-cost discipline.

## 16. Harbor Fitness qualification

The accumulated Chapters 6–10 evidence establishes a policy-dependent membership-freeze workflow with multi-system manual work. Chapter 10's $1,335–$4,312.50 direct-labor range is estimated/hypothetical, not measured; it concludes `MORE_EVIDENCE_REQUIRED`. Priority, urgency, implementation authority, budget/capacity, and platform constraints remain unresolved. Local Works fit is strong and there is no observed hard disqualifier, but those positives do not erase the gaps.

The Chapter 11 decision remains **`MORE_EVIDENCE_REQUIRED`**, with approximately five fictional planning hours for a management qualification conversation, bounded evidence sample, and limited vendor research. Reassess before design. This is deliberately not a forced advance.

## 18. Part III conclusion

Discovery can stop a problem that is not real. Reconstruction can reframe a misunderstood problem. Economics can stop trivial burden. Qualification can stop or nurture a real and meaningful problem because priority, authority, feasibility, fit, or risk fails. Only survivors enter **Part IV — Design the Right Solution**. Chapter 12 is intentionally deferred.

## 20. Readiness checkpoint

The reader should be able to explain:

- why a real problem and meaningful economics can still describe a poor opportunity;
- why customer priority, honest urgency, and reachable authority matter;
- why unknown budget is not no budget;
- why technical possibility differs from organizational and economic feasibility;
- why measurability and Local Works fit matter;
- why hard disqualifiers override positive signals;
- how estimated remaining pre-sales work protects scarce owner time;
- why qualification chooses no solution, approves no project, creates no proposal, and guarantees no sale; and
- why stopping before proposal work can be a successful outcome.

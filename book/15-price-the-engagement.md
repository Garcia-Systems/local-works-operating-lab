# Chapter 15 — Price the Engagement

**Part IV — Design the Right Solution**

> **Core question:** What should Local Works charge so the customer can reasonably justify the investment and Local Works can deliver with healthy economics?

Chapter 13 tested solution economics and Chapter 14 bounded scope. This chapter moves from delivery and owner-cost hypotheses to customer price, contribution, and a pricing decision. It does not produce a proposal.

## 1. Price must work for both sides

Price is not merely cost plus markup. An underpriced engagement can be wonderful for the customer while failing to pay for delivery, owner attention, coordination, risk, support exposure, and opportunity cost. An overpriced one can show an impressive internal margin while leaving the customer unable to justify it. The aim is not maximum price. It is a defensible, healthy deal joining **customer economics** and **Local Works economics**.

## 2. Keep the numbers separate

Current burden describes the customer's present problem. Recoverable value is the evidenced portion a solution might actually realize. Price is what the customer would pay. Delivery cost, other direct cost, and owner time are separate Local Works inputs. Contribution is price less direct project costs. Customer ROI/payback belongs to the customer view.

For example, $20,000 current burden, $8,000 recoverable value, $5,000 price, $2,000 delivery, and $300 other direct cost produce $2,700 contribution. None is interchangeable. UNKNOWN is not zero; an estimate is not a quote.

## 3. Local Works direct cost

Attribute delivery partner, project software/tool, contractor, specialist, travel, and other project-specific costs. Do not silently call general insurance, rent, taxes, or portfolio overhead “direct project cost.” Portfolio overhead matters later, but category integrity matters now.

## 4. Owner time is not free

Estimate follow-up, clarification, proposal preparation, partner coordination, customer meetings, requirements translation, QA, project management, acceptance coordination, issue resolution, and administration. Multiply expected hours by an assumed owner-hour internal planning value. Twenty hours at $75 implies $1,500. This is not necessarily a customer-visible rate.

## 5. Contribution

**Project contribution = price − delivery partner cost − other direct project costs.** Divide by positive customer price for contribution margin. Then calculate **contribution after owner-time value** by subtracting imputed owner time; its margin can also be compared. Neither is accounting profit: acquisition, overhead, insurance, tax, and other company costs remain. There is no universal required margin in this lab.

## 6. Customer economics

Bring forward Chapter 13's recoverable annual value, recurring solution cost, new operating burden, timing, confidence, and alternatives. Replace preliminary implementation cost with the actual candidate customer price. If price is $5,000 and annual net benefit after recurring costs is $6,000, simple payback is 10 months; at $10,000 it is 20 months. Show first-year and multi-year results. Do not ask how much theoretical value can be extracted; ask whether this price remains sensible after uncertainty, customer effort, downside, alternatives, and time to benefit.

## 7. The economic pricing window

A practical Local Works floor can equal expected delivery and other direct costs plus imputed owner time and an explicitly chosen minimum contribution. The input must be explained, not disguised as an industry rule. Covering a contractor invoice alone gives no economic reason to accept owner work and risk.

The customer ceiling is a supported limit based on customer-specific payback/downside guardrails and evidence—not one dollar below recoverable value. The overlap is the **practical pricing window**. A $4,000 floor and $7,000 ceiling form a $4,000–$7,000 window. It does not automatically select a price.

## 8. When no healthy deal exists

If the customer can justify $3,000 while delivery alone costs $4,000 and Local Works needs further contribution, no healthy price exists. Reduce scope, simplify the solution, find a lower-cost path, phase, let the customer implement internally, leave alone, or walk away. Never force an overlap.

## 9. Fixed fee

Fixed fee fits reasonably clear scope, known dependencies, clear acceptance, and manageable uncertainty that can be responsibly priced. It gives customer certainty and rewards efficient delivery. Scope creep, hidden dependencies, underestimated effort, and rework remain risks. Fixed fee is not inherently best.

## 10. Time and materials

T&M fits ongoing discovery, technical investigation, evolving requirements, and customers able to accept variable spend. Rate times actual effort is still accompanied by an expected range, budget guardrail, reporting, and approval boundary. This chapter does not implement invoicing.

## 11. Phased pricing

A $750 capability-validation phase, $2,500 bounded implementation, and optional integration priced only if needed can be more honest than a speculative whole-project commitment. Paid validation reduces customer downside, Local Works estimation risk, and premature custom-building.

## 12. Retainers, subscriptions, and hybrids

A retainer provides recurring access to agreed capacity/services. A subscription provides a defined recurring service. Recurrence must correspond to recurring service and value, not a desire to manufacture recurring revenue. Hybrids can join discovery and fixed implementation or implementation and later support. Full support pricing remains future work.

## 13. Price versus contractor estimate

A $3,000 partner estimate and $5,500 customer price are different. The difference is not automatically arbitrary “markup”: Local Works owns acquisition, discovery, design, scoping, communication, coordination, QA, risk, and accountability. Yet those responsibilities do not excuse a price the customer's economics cannot carry. Internal cost stays internal unless disclosure is deliberate; do not justify price by narrating a contractor invoice.

## 14. Estimation risk

A $3,000 expected cost with a $2,500–$4,000 range is explicit uncertainty. Do not blindly add 30%. Narrow scope, validate technology, phase, use T&M, or state contingency. **Uncertainty should change deal structure**, not hide in an unexplained inflated price. Track scope, delivery cost, value, payment, timing, dependency, support, change, and other risks with severity, evidence, and mitigation.

## 15. Deposits and payment timing

Upfront, deposit-plus-final, milestones, monthly, and on-completion schedules affect commitment, startup funding, contractor timing, and risk. A deposit can reserve capacity and reduce Local Works' financing burden. It is not necessarily revenue recognition; this is not accounting advice. No split works universally.

## 16. Cash exposure

A project can contribute positively yet run out of cash. If an $8,000 completion payment follows a $4,000 upfront partner bill, Local Works finances $4,000. A 50% deposit may fund it. Model receipts and project payments over stages; the largest cumulative deficit is **maximum Local Works cash exposure**.

## 17. Discount mathematics

At $6,000 price and $3,000 direct cost, contribution is $3,000. A 10% discount makes price $5,400 and contribution $2,400. Revenue fell 10%; contribution fell 20%. Test 5%, 10%, and 20% rather than treating discounts as harmless.

## 18. Price negotiation versus scope negotiation

“Do it for less” can mean lower price with unchanged scope, reduced scope, a different implementation path, phases, different payment timing, or decline. Lower price does not automatically remove work; removed work does not mechanically dictate price or value. Chapter 16 will address the broader negotiation conversation.

## 19. Bad cost-plus pricing

Adding 20% to a $4,000 contractor estimate to reach $4,800 ignores owner effort, acquisition, customer economics, scope, risk, and support. Cost matters as a constraint, not a complete pricing theory.

## 20. Bad “value extraction” pricing

Charging $45,000 merely because current burden is $50,000 ignores recoverability, confidence, customer risk and effort, time, simpler alternatives, and context. Value establishes a customer-side decision, not permission to capture nearly all benefit.

## 21. Harbor Fitness pricing

The fictional exercise carries $18,600 current burden, $9,600 hypothesized recoverable annual value, and $1,200 recurring cost. Preliminary Local Works inputs are $2,800 delivery, $400 other direct, 24 owner hours at a $75 internal value, and $800 explicitly selected required contribution. That creates a $5,800 floor. A customer-specific hypothetical 12-month-payback ceiling is $8,400.

A $6,000 candidate yields $2,800 direct contribution (46.7%), $1,000 after owner time, $8,400 annual customer net benefit, 8.6-month payback, and $2,400/$10,800/$19,200 customer results after one/two/three years. A 50% kickoff deposit reduces simplified maximum cash exposure from $3,200 to zero. These are hypotheses, not results or a quote.

Although the candidate overlaps, unvalidated platform and delivery assumptions favor **PHASE_PROJECT**: $900 validation then an optional refreshed $5,100 implementation. The artifact also shows reduced scope and a no-window case.

## 22. Healthy-deal test

On the customer side, seek meaningful expected benefit, acceptable payback, manageable downside, reasonable confidence, and sensible comparison with alternatives. On Local Works' side, cover direct costs, recognize owner effort, retain positive contribution, and manage risk, cash exposure, and responsibility. If either side fails, restructure or choose no deal.

Available outcomes are `HEALTHY_PRICE_IDENTIFIED`, `RESTRUCTURE_SCOPE`, `PHASE_PROJECT`, `NEED_BETTER_COST_ESTIMATE`, `NEED_BETTER_VALUE_EVIDENCE`, `NO_HEALTHY_PRICE`, and `DECLINE`. None issues a proposal or guarantees a sale.

# Chapter 29 — Recurring Support Economics

## Part VII — Own the Relationship After Launch

> **Core question:** Can Local Works offer useful, fair, profitable support without creating an unmanageable second job?

> **Central principle:** Recurring revenue is not automatically good revenue.

## 1. Why recurring support is different

Projects are bounded and milestone-led; support is open-ended, uncertain, interruption-led, and relationship-heavy. A profitable delivery can acquire an unprofitable support tail.

## 2. Recurring revenue is not automatically good

MRR is healthy only when customer continuity is useful, boundaries are understood, risk is tolerable, dependencies are workable, capacity exists, and economics survive owner time. Support need not be a product.

## 3. Support demand profiles

Profile requests, incidents, owner and partner hours, vendor coordination, training, configuration, documentation, after-hours demand, severity, recurrence, seasonality, and uncertainty. Preserve `UNKNOWN` when history cannot support an estimate.

## 4. Request mix

Counts conceal effort. Ten short how-to questions differ from ten vendor escalations. Categorize how-to, configuration, troubleshooting, incident/vendor/partner coordination, documentation, training, administration, reporting, and small changes; route enhancements outward.

## 5. Fixed vs variable cost

Fixed cost includes tools, documentation upkeep, account review, communication, and partner readiness. Variable cost follows tickets, incidents, partner effort, vendor calls, and after-hours events. Keep both visible.

## 6. Owner time

Track triage, communication, diagnosis, coordination, QA/retest, education, administration, documentation, meetings, and after-hours interruption. Owner availability is finite.

## 7. Interruption cost

A 15-minute request can disrupt sales, delivery, learning, or family time. Use LOW/MODERATE/HIGH where a numerical factor would imply false precision.

## 8. Delivery-partner cost

Record hourly, prepaid, retainer, per-incident, warranty-included, vendor-included, or unknown arrangements. Separate hours and cash.

## 9. Partner availability

Low rates do not guarantee incident availability. Response reliability, after-hours ability, continuity, and replacement difficulty limit safe promises.

## 10. Vendor coordination burden

A vendor may correct its issue without charge while Local Works spends real owner time gathering evidence, escalating, translating, and updating the customer.

## 11. Support revenue models

Compare monthly flat fee, prepaid hours, pay-as-you-go, incident fee, hybrid, and none. No model wins universally.

## 12. Warranty separation

Do not sell defect correction twice. Keep warranty owner/partner effort outside paid usage; high warranty burden points toward quality review, not a support-price increase.

## 13. Included support

Define eligible request types, owner and partner capacity, incidents, vendor help, training, configuration, documentation, reviews, and after-hours expectations. Avoid vague promises.

## 14. Exclusions

Fair exclusions protect new workflows, integrations, redesign, migration, major reports, third-party licenses, unsupported systems, large training, customer-caused repeats, after-hours work, and discovery as project work.

## 15. Unlimited support

Uncertain demand gives unlimited cost no natural ceiling. Unlimited is not logically impossible, but requires unusually strong usage, capacity, concurrency, and pricing evidence.

## 16. Included hours

Hours can be an internal capacity boundary without becoming the customer value story. Explain outcomes and continuity while retaining a real ceiling.

## 17. Rollover and overage

Choose no, limited, full, or not-applicable rollover deliberately. Make overage billable, quoted, deferred, new-project, explicit goodwill, or unsupported—never silently unlimited.

## 18. Pricing floor

Cover expected partner and other direct costs, administrative burden, an incident-risk allowance, imputed owner time, and reasonable contribution. Competitor guessing is not a cost model.

## 19. Support contribution

Monthly contribution equals revenue minus partner and other direct costs. Preserve this direct view rather than hiding it in a fully loaded number.

## 20. Owner-time-adjusted contribution

Subtract owner hours times an explicit imputed value. This view can reveal that attractive direct margin is poor owner economics.

## 21. Contribution per owner hour

Divide direct contribution by owner support hours, returning not applicable at zero hours. Compare with the owner's alternatives rather than declaring a universal threshold.

## 22. Opportunity cost

Support can displace sales, project delivery, education, business development, focus, and family time. Make displacement visible without pretending every consequence has a precise price.

## 23. Support capacity

Start with hours truly available across the portfolio. Compare planned support, customer count, after-hours ability, and resulting HEALTHY, BUSY, STRAINED, OVER_CAPACITY, or UNKNOWN state.

## 24. Incident reserve

Subtract a deliberate incident buffer before committing routine capacity. Scheduling 100 percent to expected work assumes surprises never happen.

## 25. Utilization

Used divided by available capacity is a signal, not an ideal percentage. Interpret it alongside mix, interruption, severity, and reserve.

## 26. Concurrency

Two customers can need urgent help together. Reuse incident thinking: priorities, communication, partner routing, and reserve must work under simultaneous demand.

## 27. After-hours economics

Availability itself costs attention and constrains life even without an event. Price and staff the commitment separately; never imply 24/7 casually.

## 28. Customer value

Value can be knowing whom to call, faster recovery, less internal troubleshooting, vendor help, documentation continuity, stable ownership, and avoiding an emergency contractor search. Seller margin does not prove buyer value.

## 29. Preventive support

Documentation, configuration, dependency, workflow, QA, and account reviews belong only if they prevent real friction. Do not manufacture busywork to justify a subscription.

## 30. Customer reviews

A lightweight review asks what happened, repeated, changed, should improve, and belongs in discovery. It makes value visible without turning the meeting into an upsell.

## 31. Break-even

Compute owner-hour and partner-hour sensitivities from revenue remaining after other costs. Usage break-even depends on request mix. Label assumptions rather than claim actuarial precision.

## 32. Risk-adjusted economics

Test low, expected, high, incident, and severe-incident demand. Expected value alone hides tail months and operational failure.

## 33. Monthly variability

One, twelve, then three hours is plausible. Multiple simulated months are more honest than treating one average month as guaranteed.

## 34. Annual economics

Aggregate twelve explicit months for revenue, direct cost, owner hours, contribution, adjusted contribution, and incidents. Do not multiply one launch month unless calling it an estimate.

## 35. Customer-level economics

Inspect each customer's revenue, partner cost, owner hours, incidents, contribution per owner hour, burden, and relationship value before averaging.

## 36. Support concentration

Revenue concentration and burden concentration differ. A customer can provide 20 percent of revenue and consume 60 percent of support hours.

## 37. Goodwill burden

Keep no-charge owner and partner work separate. Occasional judgment can be fair; recurrence signals an unpaid plan.

## 38. Warranty burden

Warranty effort belongs to delivery quality and responsibility. Hiding it as paid support obscures both quality and economics.

## 39. Vendor burden

Heavy coordination may justify a boundary, customer vendor contract, different solution, adjusted fee, or declined support—not automatic blame.

## 40. Pay-as-you-go

PAYG lowers commitment and fits low demand, but creates variable revenue, approval delay, and possible reluctance to ask for help.

## 41. Prepaid hours

Prepayment bounds capacity and payment while allowing varied work. It also creates hour accounting, rollover arguments, allowance ambiguity, and incident exhaustion.

## 42. Monthly plans

Monthly plans support continuity, predictable routing, and revenue, but risk unlimited expectations, underpricing, idle-value questions, and interruption load.

## 43. Hybrid plans

A base fee can cover bounded routine coordination while partner work and new projects remain separately approved. Explicit interfaces matter more than the label.

## 44. Support verdicts

Permit viable, bounded, higher-price, lower-scope, PAYG/prepaid/vendor better, not economic, not operational, and insufficient-evidence outcomes. Never force recurrence.

## 45. Harbor Fitness options

Chapter 27 supplies a warranty defect, how-to/configuration help, vendor coordination, one incident route, goodwill, and owner burden. Because Chapter 28 is absent from this checkout, detailed incident/monthly evidence is explicitly assumed. PAYG, Light, and Managed options use fictional prices.

## 46. Failure: cheap unlimited support

$199 against 12 owner hours, four partner hours, and two incidents becomes negative after owner time. **UNLIMITED SUPPORT CAN TURN MRR INTO NEGATIVE MARGIN.**

## 47. Failure: ignore owner time

$600 less $100 appears to contribute $500. Twelve owner hours at $75 consume $900 of capacity value, showing why direct margin alone misleads.

## 48. Failure: no incident buffer

Twenty routine hours in twenty available hours leaves nowhere for an incident. **EXPECTED DEMAND IS NOT MAXIMUM DEMAND.**

## 49. Failure: 24/7 expectation

“We're always here” invites weekend response without pricing availability, interruption, or partner coverage. **AVAILABILITY ITSELF HAS COST.**

## 50. Failure: everything included

Absorbing a new cancellation workflow alongside training, reports, configuration, calls, and incidents turns support into unpaid discovery and delivery. Boundaries protect project work.

## 51. Failure: low usage equals value

A customer paying $1,000 while receiving almost nothing may question the plan. A seller's low delivery cost is not customer value.

## 52. Failure: partner unavailable

An incident promise dependent on a partner who disappears for a week is not operationally deliverable, regardless of expected margin.

## 53. Failure: one customer eats capacity

Portfolio averages hide a customer with 20 percent of revenue and 60 percent of hours. Compare revenue and burden concentration.

## 54. Success: bounded support

Healthy support has clear inclusion/exclusion, business hours, realistic incident coordination, known partner costs, visible goodwill, project routing, reserve capacity, positive busy-month economics, and understandable continuity.
